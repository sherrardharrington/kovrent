Succession

Succession isn't a someday problem.

Cerulli projects that more than a third of advisors will retire within the next ten years, and most independent RIAs have no written succession plan. The gap is already priced into your firm: buyers, lenders, and sharp clients all read it as risk. Closing it starts with naming who runs the firm when you step back.
Last updated: August 2026
Every buyer, every lender, and every sharp client already reads your firm through one question: what happens when you step back. Here's how to get ahead of it.
Scroll
If There's No Plan
A founder's death without a plan is a fire sale with a funeral attached.
Clients start leaving in the first ninety days, and the best ones leave first. The SEC expects a continuity arrangement, and an estate scrambling to run a fiduciary business is a regulatory problem, not just a family one. Whatever the firm was worth before, a forced sale prices in the panic.
Disability is the same story on a slower clock. The firm keeps billing while the judgment that built it is gone.
Why Internal Buyouts Fail
Your G2 loves the firm. They can't finance it.
Internal buyouts need young partners with money they don't have, buying equity that gets more expensive every year the firm grows. Waiting makes the firm worth more and the internal sale less possible. Success itself closes the exit everyone said they wanted.
Seller-financed notes stretch the founder's risk into their seventies. Bank debt puts a covenant between your successor and your clients. Neither is a plan. Both are a hope with paperwork.
A Real Plan
A named successor, with terms in writing.
A funded continuity agreement for death and disability.
Equity that moves on a schedule, not a promise.
A valuation refreshed every two years, by someone with no stake in the answer.

The Compounding Part
The wealth transfer isn't only happening to advisors. It's happening to their clients at the same time. Your book is aging with you, and when a client dies, the money moves to heirs who already have their own advisor, in their own city, on their own apps.
A firm with no continuity plan loses twice. It loses clients at the founder's transition, and it loses the next generation of every client family it never engaged. Both losses are priced into what a buyer will pay, years before either happens.
The founders who do well in this decade are the ones who treat the handoff as an asset to build, not an event to survive.

What Is My RIA Worth

What your RIA is actually worth.

RIA valuations are typically expressed three ways: a percentage of AUM, a multiple of revenue, or a multiple of EBITDA, and the honest answer before diligence is a range, not a number. Kovrent provides a real range within two weeks of reviewing three years of financials.
Last updated: August 2026
Three ways buyers price an RIA, and the five things that move the number. Educational, not an offer.
Scroll
The Three Lenses
01
Percent of AUM
The oldest shorthand and the least useful on its own. Two firms with identical AUM can earn very different revenue from it. Buyers use it to sanity-check, not to price.
02
Revenue multiple
Better. It prices what clients actually pay you, and it rewards recurring, fee-based revenue over transactional income. Growth and client retention push it up. Concentration pushes it down.
03
EBITDA multiple
The one serious buyers underwrite. It prices what's left after the business pays for itself, including a market salary for you. If you've never paid yourself a market salary on paper, this number will surprise you.
What Moves the Number
How much revenue is recurring and fee-based.
Client ages, and how concentrated the top ten are.
Growth net of market movement. Organic growth is the tell.
How much revenue walks out the door if the founder does.
Margins, after a market-rate salary for everyone who works there.

Three Models

Private equity, consolidator, or permanent capital.

A private equity fund typically resells your firm within five to seven years. A consolidator typically retires your brand. A permanent holding company buys to hold, which changes what happens to your name, your team, and your clients after closing.
Last updated: August 2026

All three buy RIAs. All three are the right answer for somebody. The differences that matter are the hold period, who owns the client relationship afterward, and what forces a second sale. Below is the honest version, including where we are the wrong buyer.

Side by Side
  Private equity fund Consolidator Permanent holding company
{{ row.label }} {{ row.pe }} {{ row.cons }} {{ row.perm }}
Definitions

Private equity fund

A pool of outside capital raised with a stated life. The manager is obligated to return that capital, so every business it buys is bought with an exit in mind. That is not a criticism, it is the job. It means the question is never whether your firm gets sold again, only when and to whom.

Consolidator

A national advisory firm that acquires practices and merges them into one operating company: usually one brand, one service model, one investment platform. Done well, it gives clients depth a small firm cannot afford on its own. What you trade is the thing that made your firm distinctive.

Permanent holding company

A company that buys firms to own them, funded by capital with no return-by date. It grows slower, pays less at the top of a cycle, and depends entirely on whose money sits behind it. No fund behind us means no fund clock. Ask, and a partner will walk you through the capital structure line by line.

What the market looked like last year

Echelon Partners counted 466 announced wealth management transactions in 2025, up 27.3% over 2024, including a record 185 deals involving firms with at least $1 billion in assets. Private equity backed buyers accounted for 56.8% of those billion-dollar deals.
